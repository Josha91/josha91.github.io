Many people have heard of the pop-science result that above a certain threshold, more money does not make you happier. In fact, I read a story once of the CEO of a company who was so convinced by this result that he decided to lower his own salary, and raise that of every single one of his employees to that threshold, to maximize happiness.

Apocryphal or not, an inspiring story. And should we all be more like this CEO? After all, how do you justify earning more? Perhaps you could still accept your astronomical salary, but give everything above 70k p.a. away?

Let's examine the science behind this story.

## Origins of the claim
The original study is _High income improves evaluation of life but notemotional well-being_ by Angus Deaton and Daniel Kahneman. In this, they use data from the Gallup-Healthways Well-Being Index to compare income to two things: emotional wellbeing (that is, day-to-day happiness) and long-term life satisfaction. What they find is that the former does not increase above \$75,000 whereas the latter _does_. Intuitively, this makes sense: at a certain point, there are only so many extra luxury goods you can buy to improve your day-to-day, but if you take stock of your life you inevitably think about how successful you are, which often correlates with income.

As an aside, because I thought it was interesting: a methodological flaw they point out in previous studies is comparing income directly to these happiness markers, rather than the _logarithm_ as they do here. According to _Weber's law_, the logarithm tracks people's feeling better because it is the relative, rather than absolute, difference that matters.

Here, "ladder" refers to the life satisfaction and, as can be seen, it increases with income. The other measures all refer to different forms of day-to-day happiness, and all increase only up to a certain point, after which they flatten off. Note, in today's (2024) dollars, that would be closer to 100k US dollars.

## Maybe the plateau doesn't exist after all?
Later work, most notably _Experienced well-being rises with income, even above $75,000 per year_ by Matthew Killingworth, finds that the plateau does not, in fact, exist. Methodologically, the biggest difference with the original study is (1) more fine grained income statements (especially, higher resolution at the high-earner end), reducing statistical artifacts, and (2) asking (via smart phone pop ups) in _real time_ about people's happiness, and scoring that on a 0-10 scale rather than binary yes/no questions. In what the authors rather amusingly describe as a _adversarial collaboration_, Kahneman and Killingworth also produced a 2023 paper in which they reconcile their results and find that even though Killingworth's results describe the majority of people, for a minority (the least happy people) the original result (with a plateau) still holds.

## Conclusions
By and large, the claim is false, and certainly the number of 70,000 / 75,000 that lives rent-free in many people's head is both outdated and wrong. There _is_ marginal happiness to be found above that.

However, because of the nature of the logarithmic income-axis, the extra happiness comes at a higher and higher price, and so asking yourself whether that is really necessary and whether your extra happiness is worth that of 10 poor people elsewhere is still a justified question that moral philosophers might have a thing or two to say about.
