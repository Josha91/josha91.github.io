# Do men earn more because there are more male geniuses?
A claim I have often heard is that while yes, the gender pay gap exists, this is not because of sexism and not because men are _smarter_ or _better_, but because men are more _variable_. That is, while the average IQ might be similar, the proportion of very intelligent (and very unintelligent) men is higher than in women.

Importantly, these claims are typically not made by people who would self-identify as a misogynist. Likely they aren't. This **greater male variability hypothesis** is just a very compelling explanation for observed inequalities, a competitor to the prevailing "narrative" of sexism and unequal opportunities (and, conveniently, an explanation that does not require further corrections or systematic changes to the status quo).

That is the not the say that the claims are untrue, but it is a reason to be cautious. Therefore, let's delve into the literature and see what is real and what is fiction.

## Equal means, different details
Even though there are inconsistent, contradictory results in the literature, the general consensus appears to be that men and women do not have significantly different levels of general intelligence (e.g. [Gioffre et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-022-09705-1?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [Gioffre et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11541283/)). This is expected, since the second order moment would not be invoked to explain gender disparity if the first order moment (the mean) would suffice as per the discussion above.

It is often found that men and women excel in different _types_ of tasks: males tend to do better on (some) spatial tasks, while women tend to perform better on verbal tasks.

Most importantly, yes, there is significant evidence for men having a higher variability, even at the same mean, and not just in general intelligence but in a variety of tasks and biological features (e.g. [Wierenga et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8675415/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)). On general intelligence, [Arden & Plomin 2006](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191886906000420) for instance find a variance ratio (male-to-female) of between 1.06 and 1.21. To get a sense of what a difference that makes, I took the larger of these values and plotted two Gaussians:

In numbers, this would get you 1.5 men at "gifted" intelligence (130+) and as much as 4:1 at a genius range of 160+ for the higher end of the obtained variability range. Taking the lower end ratio (1.06), we get 14% and 61% extra males above 130 and 160, respectively.

An interesting follow-up question is, of course, whether this is _nurture_ or _nature_ (or, as is often the case when asking that question, a bit of both). Evidence here seems very mixed. On the one hand, researchers point out that the difference is seen early on, and genetic reasons are offered for observed difference (such as averaging of phenotypes in homogametic subjects (i.e. females) compared to heterogametic subjects (i.e. males)). On the other hand, great variability is found in the magnitude of this variability between different countries and subgroups, and increased female participation in a given task is found to reduce the effect as well.

So, in answer to the question we set out to investigate: yes, it appears that men seem to display higher variance than women on a variety of traites.

## Does this explain gender disparities?
According to [O'Dea et al. 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-06292-0?utm_source=chatgpt.com) that, while the greater male variability is replicated, it is insufficient to explain the male overrepresentation in STEM (and in fact they find the effect to be _smaller_ in STEM subjects than other subjects). Other facts, such as preferences and social norms, matter too.

Differences are also found in social attitudes and risk taking [Thoni & Volk 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8201935/?utm_source=chatgpt.com), though no explicit mention is made on the impact on economic and sociatal outcomes.

I could not find any papers directly addressing the affect of the GMV effect on the gender wage gap specifically, but based on what I have read, my cautious conclusion would be that it _is_ a relevant factor, it should be taken into account when studying the gender wage gap and other inequalities in outcome. That is not to say that it explain it all, it doesn't and we know of plenty of other factors that are important.
