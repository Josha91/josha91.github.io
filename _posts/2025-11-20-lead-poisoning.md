Did lead poisoning lead to the demise of the Roman Empire? I have heard this claim many times, but I do not know the veracity of the claim. Is it an urban myth? Is it a fringe scientific theory that gained notoriety becuase it's so compelling? Or is it actually based on firm scientific consensus with a robust body of evidence? In this post, I'd like to find out.

## The proposed mechanism
How would this work? First, let's clarify what lead poisoning is. Lead poisoning can happen by breathing in contaminated air, drinking contaminated liquids or ingesting contaminated substances. So, in many ways. Until very recently, it was for instance used in turmeric powder in Bangladesh, to create an intense yellow color; it has been used in paints, leading to high levels of exposure; and perhaps most famously, until the 1970s, when it was discovered there are no safe levels of lead and that leaded petrol became outlawed in much of the Western world. According to the WHO, as of 2021 some 1.5 _million_ deaths were attributed to lead exposure world wide (related to cardiovascular effects). For reference, some 60 million people die every year, which would mean 5% of all deaths were attributed to lead exposure in 2021 - a staggering amount!

In Roman times, exposure is thought to have come primarily through three channels:
(1) Lead water pipes, providing especially high class Romans with water
(2) Lead-coated cooking pots, again used by high society primarily
(3) A background level of lead due to intense mining activity. This was mostly due to silver mining (for coins), which released a lot of lead as a byproduct.

Intriguingly, the Romans to some extend seem to have known about lead poisoning. Vitruvius, in the first century B.C. writes:

> This we can exemplify from plumbers, since in them the natural colour of the body is replaced by a deep pallor. For when lead is smelted in casting, the fumes from it settle upon their members, and day after day burn out and take away all the virtues of the blood from their limbs. Hence, water ought by no means to be conducted in lead pipes, if we want to have it wholesome. That the taste is better when it comes from clay pipes may be proved by everyday life, for though our tables are loaded with silver vessels, yet everybody uses earthenware for the sake of purity of taste.

Pliny the Elder makes similar observations.

## What does the evidence say?
Point one is perhaps the most famous of these. In [a 2014 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4020092/pdf/pnas.201400097.pdf) this was investigated, and while they found the lead levels must have been significantly higher in Roman "tap" water than water that did _not_ pass through the pipe system, it was judged to be unlikely to have a significant effect on the health, at least to the point of having history-changing consequences.

The second suggestion is lead cooking ware poisoning food. In particular, _sapa_ or _defrutum_ is [mentioned](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/encyclopaedia_romana/wine/leadpoisoning.html), a grape-based syrup. Because of the acidic grapes, the lead would dissolve creating lead acetate. Wine was sweetened with this, leading to the alleged poisoning and creating a strong bias for poisoning predominantely the wine-indulgent elites. This likely lead to symptoms like kidney failure, gout, and cognitive decline. Enough to explain individual cases of mild to severe poisoning, but not enough for societal-level collapse.

In an intriguing [new study](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2419630121), point (3) was investigated using ice cores. The estimated background levels of lead in the air (that is, the _average_ European levels - this was for sure more pronounced near mines) was so high that cognitive decline might have been as much as 2.5-3 IQ points in the Pax Romana era. A lot, to be sure, but not enough to explain the decline of an empire. In particular, it has been pointed out that the Empire was hugely successful and productive _especially_ when lead production was high. Furthermore, competing nations would suffer from the same background exposure.

## Conclusion
From my reading, it seems most scholars agree it is unlikely that lead poisoning alone lead to the collapse of the Roman Empire. At most it may have been a (minor) contributing factor, but even that is contested.
