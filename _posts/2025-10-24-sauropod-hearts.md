While reading a book about dinosaurs recently, I got interested in the question of how Sauropods got to be so large. I will probably write a follow-up post on this, but first: a theory that is probably not correct, but super interesting.

The paper I will discuss below is [Choy & Altman, 1992](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/014067369291722K). The paper is concerned with one problematic aspect of gigantism: how did blood circulation work in animals as big as Sauropods? In particular, if the Sauropod neck is habitually kept upright (on which opinions differ(ed)), blood would need to be pumped up some 12 meters! The implied systolic pressure (the force on the artery walls when the heart beats) required would be absolutely staggering. Whereas the typical human systolic pressure is about 120 mm Hg, the highest found in nature today is ~330 mm Hg in, not coincidentally, giraffes. The estimate for this long-necked Sauropod would be 880 mm Hg!

This, in turn, increases the chance of arterial rupture and thus death.

The suggested solution to this problem by Choy & Altman is rather intriguing: not one giant, central heart, but rather a series of hearts. This is sketched in the figure below:

![img](/img/choy_altman.png)

By having several pumps along the neck, each pump could be less powerful, lowering the pressure on the arterical walls.

While intriguing, the authors acknowledge that it is, at the moment, not really testable because soft tissue is not preserved and there are no e.g. osteological correlates. Another major issue of the theory is that no other vertebral animals are known, past or present, with multiple hearts. Some examples are known, such as octopuses, squid and some types of worms, but those are much further removed in the evolutionary tree.

This theory is therefore not currently favoured. A more popular model speculates a single (but gigantic: possibly hundreds of kilograms) heart, specialized vasculature (much like a giraffe, with thick arterial walls and valves to prevent backflow), and perhaps most importantly, a neck that is predominantly held at or slighlyt above shoulder height, rather than vertically like a giraffe.
