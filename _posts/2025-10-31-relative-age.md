I read once that ice hockey players that were born around march were disproportionately (i.e. more than expected based on random chance) succesfull as measured by the number of professional players as a function of birth date.
This always stuck with me, and wondered what else this may extend to - other sports? And how about academic pursuits? Especially now that I have a child, for who this might one day be relevant, I wanted to look further into this.

## The relative age effect
This effect actually has a name: the _relative age effect_, also known as the _birthdate effect_. The original finding I had read about is the study _Hockey success and birthdate: The relative age effect_ by Barnsley et al. (1985). They investigate the distribution of NHL player birthdays by quartile. Their key figure is reproduced below:

![img](/img/relative_age_effect/barnsley1985.png)

The effect had already been observed before that however in an education context in the '60s, and has been widely studied since as well. It has been observed in a large number of disciplines, and it has been shown to correlate with how popular a sport is (more popular sports showing stronger RAE) and at what level the sport is played (higher levels showing stronger RAE).

Interestingly, there are also findings that while the RAE exists for right handed players in tennis (the majority), the effect does _not_ exist for left handed players [Loffing et al. 2010](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13598139.2010.488084). The suggestion is that left-handedness gives a competitive advantage that compensates for the RAE. 

To see this for myself, I compiled the birth dates of all current players in the Dutch Eredivisie (N=498). Besides making me feel old (less than 5% of players are younger than me!), this neatly shows the RAE: there are almost _double_ the amount of players born in Q1 compared to Q4:

![img](/img/relative_age_effect/eredivisie_rae.png)

## Why does this happen?

The most common and most obvious explanation for this is the "maturation-selection hypothesis": within a selection year, if you are a few months older than most of your peers that gives you a significant advantage because you have matured more, physically and psychologically, to a non-negligble degree.

The effect is strongest in childhood, when athletes are still growing and developing. The effect is thought to decrease (or even, according to some studies, reverse) over time as kids mature and transition into senior categories.

Besides the physical advantages, psychological effects are also at play. For instance, relatively older players may believe themselves to be truly more talented (or may be identified as such by coaches or scouts), which leads to better outcomes (the Pygmalion effect).

To the contrary, the "underdog effect" is referenced where relatively _young_ athletes are fighting an uphill battle throughout their career, leading to better outcomes if they do persevere and helps to explain the partial reversal of the RAE at the senior level.

## The relative age effect in academia
How does this translate to academia? A recent meta analysis on the matter is provided by [Urruticoechea et al. 2001](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/18/17/8966). They find that relatively younger children:

- Obtain lower scores on both cognitive and motor skills
- Have to repeat more often
- Are less socialized

Research finds that these effects are most pronounced in early childhood (primary school age) and disappears or even reverses at the high school level, much like we saw for ice hockey. However, other research finds that there _are_ effects lasting into adulthood, such as lower levels of university degrees and lower income levels for the relatively younger students.

How to understand that? One suggestion is that in early childhood, a lot of self-image and self-confidence cements itself. If you are repeatedly told you are smart and gifted, maybe even placed in a gifted program, this easily becomes a story you tell about yourself. This may last a long time, even if you start slacking in high school, while your relatively younger peer works hard and starts getting comparable (or even higher!) grades.

Even having the same high school grades, though, this self-confidence gives you the confidence to continue learning, take chances, and potentially be offered early leadership roles, for instance.

That's just one possible explanation, however. Another explanation is that in many school systems, selections are made well before the age at which the RAE is found to have attenuated. In the Netherlands, for instance, children are typically sorted in three different levels of high school by the age of 11 or 12 - well before most research finds convergence between academic results of older and younger students. While it's _possible_ to move between levels, this doesn't happen often and, in my experience, mostly for students with exceptional work ethic and perseverance. To me, the existence of the relative age effect is a condemnation of the Dutch high school system.

What to do about this? Some suggestions I have found:

- Making teachers aware of this bias, so they can adjust their expectations (though one should be careful about over-corrections)
- Smaller enrollment windows; for instance, making cohorts on a half-yearly basis will proportionally shrink the RAE
- Avoid standarized tests and uniform curricula. This avoids direct comparison, and lets students learn at their own rate. This is one of the core principles of Montessori schools.

All in all, it is very interesting to see how prevalent this effect is and how teachers and parents alike should be aware of this if we want the best for (all) our children.
